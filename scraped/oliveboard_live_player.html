Directions : Read the following passage carefully and answer the questions that follow.

For much of the late twentieth century, Chinese manufacturing was associated primarily with low-cost, low-durability goods designed to meet mass demand in price-sensitive markets. These products were often dismissed in advanced economies as functional but inferior substitutes, suitable mainly where affordability outweighed performance. Over time, however, this perception has become increasingly outdated. Chinese firms have steadily moved beyond cost-led competitiveness to invest in research, design, and systems integration, allowing them to compete in segments once dominated by Western and Japanese manufacturers.

This transformation has been driven by a deliberate alignment of industrial policy, scale advantages, and technological learning. Rather than relying solely on imitation, firms have absorbed global knowledge while adapting it to domestic conditions, producing innovations that are incremental yet cumulative. As a result, Chinese products now incorporate advanced software, data-driven optimisation, and integrated supply chains that reduce production cycles and improve reliability. The emphasis has shifted from merely producing cheaper alternatives to delivering products that combine affordability with technical sophistication.

The implications of this shift extend beyond consumer markets into strategic sectors such as renewable energy, telecommunications, and advanced manufacturing. Countries long regarded as technological leaders increasingly recognise that China is not only catching up but, in some domains, setting benchmarks. Even economies like Germany, traditionally confident in their engineering depth, express concern about the speed and coordination with which Chinese firms translate research into scalable output. This unease is not rooted solely in price competition but in the possibility that established innovation hierarchies may be reconfigured.

Western responses to this development reveal an underlying tension. On the one hand, collaboration with Chinese suppliers remains economically attractive due to efficiency gains and integrated production networks. On the other, policymakers and industries worry about dependency, standards control, and long-term competitiveness. The debate is therefore less about whether Chinese products have improved and more about how global systems should adapt to a landscape in which technological leadership is no longer geographically concentrated. Understanding this evolution requires moving beyond outdated stereotypes and acknowledging the structural capabilities that now underpin Chinese manufacturing.
